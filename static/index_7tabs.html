The mechanism behind every finding

Each finding on this site comes from a specific calculation. This page shows what was computed, why that method was chosen over a simpler one, and where each method can fail.

Foundation

The data, and what "practice data" means

What it is
One row per sponsored post: date, creator (size, niche, city, language, platform, followers), brand and category, fee, format and length, script, posting day and hour, views, likes, comments, saves, shares, link clicks, conversions, revenue. 700 creators with Indian names, cities and languages. 7,500 deals from October 2024 to September 2026.
How it was made
Generated by a program in which a few real-world effects were written in on purpose, with noise on top: overbooked creators decay, big creators give worse value, asking mid-video lifts clicks, some openings fit some categories, topic mismatch hurts, evenings get more reach. Language, city, video length and weekday were given no effect.
Why this is useful
The true answer is known, so the analysis can be graded. Every effect that was planted is recovered, and every effect that was not planted is correctly reported as absent. That shows the pipeline works. It does not show the effect sizes exist in the real market.
Finding 1

Money share against return, by creator size

Question
Does the money go where the return is?
Calculation
For each size tier, total revenue divided by total fee (a ratio of sums, so one huge deal cannot dominate the way it would in an average of per-deal ratios), next to that tier's share of all spend. A return of 1.0 on the chart is Rs 100 back for Rs 100 paid.
Why this way
A plain average of per-deal returns would be pulled up by a few lucky deals. Total over total is what a finance team would compute.
Check
Creators are resampled, not deals, because deals from one creator resemble each other. This gives the believable range of the gap between mid-sized and celebrity returns.
Limit
Fee and size move together, so this shows where money went and what came back. It does not prove that size causes the gap.

Grey bars: share of spend. Coloured bars: return per rupee. Green is above break-even, red is below.

Finding 2

Where the ask sits in the video

Question
Do clicks and sales change when the call to action comes at the start, middle or end?
Calculation
Video deals only. Median click rate and median return by ask position. The ask position is itself extracted from the script text by the script reader (the AI step).
Check
The model changes only ask position, holding everything else on the deal fixed. Result: returns change a lot, engagement does not, which is what a click-driven effect should look like.
Limit
The effect was planted strongly, and real deals will almost certainly show a smaller one.

Median click-through rate by ask position, video deals only. The return comparison is in the Findings tab.

Finding 3

Overbooked creators

Question
Does a creator who has just done many sponsored posts deliver less?
Calculation
For every deal, count how many deals that same creator did in the 30 days before it. Group deals into 0 to 1, 2, 3 and 4 or more, and compare median engagement and return.
Why this way
Counting only prior deals matters. Using the creator's total deal count would just measure popularity.
Check
The model compares 4 recent deals against 1 for an otherwise identical deal. Engagement falls 29 percent and return falls 44 percent. The creator list marks a creator as overbooked when a quarter or more of their deals came with 3+ recent deals.

Median engagement rate by number of the creator's deals in the prior 30 days.

Finding 4

Topic match

Question
Is a creator's audience less responsive when the brand is outside their usual topic?
Calculation
A deal is a match when the creator's niche equals the brand's category. Compare median engagement for matched and mismatched deals, then repeat inside the model with only that flag changed.
Result
Mismatched deals earn roughly a fifth less engagement.
Finding 5

Which opening works in which category

Question
Does the way a script opens matter, and does it depend on what is being sold?
Calculation
Each script is tagged with one of seven opening styles. Median engagement for every opening and category pair forms the heatmap. The Playground only calls options "tied" when the model puts them within a few percent of each other, so it does not claim a winner it cannot support.
Limit
Some cells hold few deals. The heatmap shows a pattern, and the model smooths it across categories.

Median engagement by opening style (rows) and brand category (columns). Darker is higher.

Finding 6

Things that did not matter

Question
Do language, city type, video length or weekday explain results?
Calculation
A raw gap can come only from creator size (big creators skew to certain languages and cities, for instance). Each deal is first divided by the typical value for its size and format, giving an index where 1.00 is typical. Then each creator is reduced to a single number and a rank test (Kruskal-Wallis) is run across groups.
Why one number per creator
A creator with 30 deals would otherwise count 30 times and make a tiny gap look significant.
Result
Language, city type, video length and weekday show no difference. Hour of posting does: evening posts return more (p below 0.001), with no engagement change. That one was caught at this stage and the page was corrected.
Under the hood

The prediction model

What it predicts
For one deal, a typical value and an 80% range for engagement rate and for return.
Method
Gradient-boosted trees fitted three times per outcome, for the 10th, 50th and 90th percentile (quantile regression). Inputs: size tier, followers, niche, platform, brand category, format, length, opening style, ask position, posting day and hour, fee, topic match and recent-deal count.
Why quantiles
A single predicted number hides how uncertain a sponsored deal is. The range says how often a plan can miss.
Honest testing
Training and test folds are split by creator, so the model is always scored on creators it has not seen. Splitting by deal would leak each creator's habits into the test and flatter the score.
Range correction
The raw 10 to 90 range held the true value only about 74% of the time. A conformal correction widened it until held-out coverage reached about 80%.
Scores
Rank correlation 0.90 for engagement and 0.73 for return. Errors are 31% and 23% smaller than guessing the average. Return is harder to predict, since sales are noisier than likes.

What the model leans on. Size and recent-deal count drive engagement. Fee and size drive return. The time of posting carries little weight, which fits a small effect.

Predicted against actual on held-out creators. Points near the dashed line mean the model is honest. The return model underpredicts at the very top, meaning it is cautious for the best deals.

What-if results. One factor is changed while all others stay fixed. This is the "holding other factors equal" column in the scorecard. The hour comparison in this earlier chart showed no engagement change. The later return check found a small positive effect for evening posts (about +9%), which the scorecard reports.

From findings to action

How a plan is built

Step 1: split by size
The model predicts returns for each size tier in the brand's category. Weights are the predicted return squared (for sales), so money leans toward better tiers without putting everything into one. Squaring is a design choice and the page does not claim it is optimal.
Step 2: caps
Tiny 15%, small 35%, mid-sized 60%, big plus celebrity 25% together (50% for reach goals, where large audiences are cheap per view). Caps stop one tier from absorbing the plan when a model estimate is off.
Step 3: pick creators
Only creators with a matching niche, 4 or more deals, not overbooked, and (for sales) a return above break-even. Ranked by a score: 60% return rank, 25% consistency, 15% not overbooked. Each creator is booked once.
Step 4: report the range
The plan's typical return comes with the range for single deals, and is compared with what the same category returned under Monk's past mix.
The AI step

Script reader

Purpose
Scripts are text. The reader turns each into two columns: opening style and ask position. After that they are analysed like any other column.
Two readers
A rule-based reader runs offline. An optional language-model reader (needs an API key) handles messy scripts. Neither has been run on real Monk scripts.
Accuracy
On templated scripts the rules got 91% of openings and 100% of ask positions right (1,500 scripts). On 24 deliberately messy scripts they got 25% of openings and 88% of ask positions. For openings, the reader says "unclear" and asks the user rather than guessing. That choice matters more than the headline accuracy.
Self-checks

Things caught and corrected while building

Posting time
First reported as having no effect. The size-adjusted test showed an effect on returns, so the claim was reversed.
Allocation caps
Big plus celebrity spend came out near 60% against a 25% cap. The selection step now trims the weakest big creators until the cap holds.
Model-quality wording
"Ranks deals correctly 90% of the time" was wrong. It was rank correlation. The page now says that.
Heatmap wording
"Brand introduction is lowest in every column" was too strong, since two columns have exceptions. It now says "at or near the bottom".

The code is plain Python: pandas for tables, scipy for tests, scikit-learn for the model, Flask for the page. Nothing here depends on a hosted model, except the optional script reader.

Evidence

How sure the data is, and how it was built

Every finding is listed here with its size, the range it could plausibly fall in, and whether it held up under checks. Things that did not hold up are listed too.

The scorecard

Believable range: the span the number moves across when each comparison is repeated 400 times on reshuffled creators. Holding other factors equal: the same comparison made by a prediction model that keeps everything else about the deal fixed.

How the work was done

1

Collect and clean

Every deal becomes one row: date, creator, brand, fee, script, views, likes, comments, clicks, sales. Duplicates and impossible numbers are removed. Nothing else is dropped. 7,500 deals in, 7,500 kept.

2

Measure what is normal

Return per Rs 100 and engagement are computed for every creator size, category, opening and ask timing, so each deal can be compared with similar deals instead of with the whole pile.

3

Test whether a gap is real

A gap between two groups can be luck. Each comparison is repeated 400 times on reshuffled data, counting each creator once instead of once per deal, because several deals from one creator are not independent.

4

Separate causes from bystanders

Big creators also tend to ask late and be booked often, so raw gaps can mislead. A prediction model changes one thing at a time, such as ask timing, and keeps everything else fixed. That produces the "holding other factors equal" column.

5

Check the model on strangers

The model was tested on creators it had never seen. Its ranking of deals from best to worst matches reality closely (rank correlation for engagement, for returns). Its errors are smaller than guessing the average for engagement and smaller for returns. The ranges it gives were calibrated until the 80% range held the true value about 80% of the time on unseen creators.

6

Turn scripts into data the AI step

Scripts are free text, so a reader (rules offline, or a language model with an API key) extracts two fields from each: how the script opens and when the ask comes. Those fields join the table and are analysed like any other column. This is the only place AI is used. Everything else is statistics. See the Script Lab tab.

Limits, stated plainly

Practice data. The 7,500 deals were generated with realistic patterns built in. The method recovering those patterns shows the pipeline works. It does not show that the same effect sizes exist in the market, and the ask-timing effect in particular was built strongly.

Association and cause. Holding other factors equal narrows the gap between "goes with" and "causes", but only a controlled test, such as alternating ask timing across similar creators, would settle it.

Wide outcomes. Individual deals vary a lot. A typical result and an 80% range are reported, and about 1 deal in 5 lands outside the range.

What would change the conclusions. Each finding gets re-run on Monk's real deals. A finding stays when its believable range excludes zero on the real data. A finding that disappears is reported as disappeared.

Glossary

Return per Rs 100 (ROAS)
Sales that came back for every Rs 100 paid to creators. Above 100 makes money.
Engagement rate
Likes, comments, saves and shares as a share of views.
Opening line (hook)
The first sentence of the video.
The ask (call to action)
The moment a creator says "use my code" or "link in bio".
Overbooked
A creator with 3 or more brand deals in the previous 30 days.
Believable range
The span a number could realistically fall in if the data had been a little different.
p-value
How likely a gap this size is to appear by chance if there were no real difference. Below 0.01 is treated as real here.
Creator size
Tiny is under about 10k followers, small up to 50k, mid-sized up to 250k, big up to 1M, celebrity above that.
